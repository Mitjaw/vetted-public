Do finance YouTubers’ stock picks beat the market?

Mostly no. Fewer than half of their buy recommendations beat the S&P 500, and buying right after a video worked no better than buying the same stocks on any other day.

Vetted reads the transcripts of finance‑YouTube videos and logs every stock a creator recommends or warns about. On this page I check those calls against simply holding the S&P 500.

After cleaning, the dataset has calls from videos on channels, March 2024 to March 2026.

Step 1 of 6 · Ask

What I wanted to know

Finance YouTube is full of stock recommendations. Vetted started as a way to rank creators by how well their calls turn out. Before building more of it, I wanted to know whether the calls say anything about what a stock does next.

The answer matters to two groups: people who watch these channels and wonder whether to act on a recommendation, and people who build rankings of creators or analysts, which is what Vetted set out to do.

Three questions

  1. Do buy recommendations beat the S&P 500 over one to twelve months?
  2. Does the call itself help (when it was made, and whether it was a buy or a warning), or do these channels just like stocks that happened to do well?
  3. Are some channels better than others, beyond what luck would produce?

Step 2 of 6 · Prepare

Where the data comes from

I collected the data myself. My pipeline downloads the transcript of every new video from a fixed list of channels. A small language model (Claude Haiku) reads each transcript, lists the stocks that come up and notes for each one whether the creator is positive or negative about it. The results go into a database. Share prices come from Yahoo Finance.

So on this page a “buy call” means: the creator spoke positively about the stock. That is true for about seven rows in ten. The negative labels are too unreliable to build a conclusion on.

How the check was done

The sample was drawn at random with a fixed seed: rows labelled as buy and labelled as avoid or sell. Each one was compared with the passage of the transcript where the stock comes up and sorted into the four groups in the chart. I did this with Claude Code, a stronger model than the one in the pipeline, using a stricter definition of “recommendation”. Nobody labelled the sample a second time, so I cannot say how much two readers would disagree. The labels are published with the analysis code so anyone can check them row by row. Step 3 of 6 · Process

Cleaning: from mentions to calls

Most of what the model extracts is a stock being mentioned in passing. Each step below removes rows that cannot answer the question. Tap a row to see why.

Four decisions that matter

Returns are counted from the day after the video

The data has the date of a video but not the time. If I used the closing price of the same day, a video published in the evening would get credit for a price move that had already happened. So the starting price is the first closing price after the video date. From there I measure 30, 90, 180 and 365 days, and I measure the S&P 500 (through the SPY fund) over exactly the same days.

Companies that went bankrupt or were bought stay in

Yahoo Finance removes companies once they stop trading. Leaving them out would quietly delete some of the worst calls. I mapped renamed companies to their new ticker and got prices for delisted ones from a second source. calls on such companies are in the data, valued at their last traded price. Some rarely mentioned tickers could not be found in either source and are missing.

Repeated calls stay in and are marked

Channels keep coming back to their favourite stocks. of the buy calls repeat a call the same channel made on the same stock in the 90 days before. A viewer sees every one of them, so they stay in. Every result is also shown with each idea counted only once.

What is in the public dataset

One row per call: channel, video ID, date, ticker, direction, the model’s original label, its confidence, how often the stock was named, and two yes/no columns for “named four or more times” and “repeat of an earlier call”. It contains no transcript text and no prices. The notebook downloads prices itself.

Step 4 of 6 · Analyze

What the data shows

All results compare a stock with the S&P 500 over the same days. “+2 pp” means the stock did two percentage points better than the index, “−2 pp” two points worse.

  1. More than half of the buy calls did worse than the S&P 500.
  2. On average the calls were ahead, thanks to a few stocks that were recommended over and over.
  3. It made no difference when a call was made, or whether it was a recommendation or a warning.
  4. No channel was clearly better than the others.

Finding 2 of 4

On average the calls were ahead, thanks to a few stocks that were recommended over and over.

Six months after a video the average buy call was percentage points ahead of the S&P 500. of that lead comes from three stocks (). These channels also prefer tech stocks that move more than the market, and the two years covered here were good years for exactly those stocks.

How to read this chart

Every row repeats the same calculation with one thing changed, for example leaving out the three most successful stocks. The dot is the result. The line around it shows how uncertain that result is (a 95% confidence interval). If the line crosses zero, the data also fit with no advantage at all. A filled dot means the line does not cross zero.

How sure is this? Fairly sure that the average is carried by a few stocks. Much less sure that there is a real advantage at all: of the four time spans I looked at, only the 180-day result for all calls is clearly above zero, and in most of the stricter versions the line crosses zero.

What each row means, and how the ranges are calculated
  • All buy calls: every call counts once, compared with the S&P 500.
  • Compared with the Nasdaq-100: the same calls against an index full of tech stocks, which is a fairer comparison for tech-heavy picks.
  • Adjusted for risk: a stock that swings twice as much as the market should also gain twice as much when the market rises. This row subtracts that expected gain. The typical recommended stock moves times as much as the market (its “beta”, measured over the 250 trading days before the call).
  • Without the three best stocks: leaves out the three tickers that added most to the total.
  • Each idea counted once: drops a call if the same channel made the same call on the same stock in the previous 90 days.
  • Only stocks discussed at length: calls where the stock is named four or more times. These have the most reliable labels.
  • Every month / every channel counts equally: averages the monthly or channel averages, so one busy month or one large channel cannot dominate.

For all buy calls the 95% intervals are to after 30 days, to after 90, to after 180 and to after 365. I did not correct these for looking at four time spans.

The calls are not independent of each other. A hundred calls on the same stock share one outcome, and all calls made in the same month share one market. The ranges account for both (standard errors clustered by ticker and by month). Treating the calls as independent would make the 90-day range too narrow, and the 365-day range too narrow.

Finding 3 of 4

It made no difference when a call was made, or whether it was a recommendation or a warning.

If the calls contained information, stocks should do better after a recommendation than after a warning. They should also do better from the day of the video than from some other day. I found neither.

The numbers behind it

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • How the calls do in a falling market. March 2024 to November 2026 was a strong period led by tech stocks.
  • Anything precise. About three in ten “buy” rows are not real recommendations, which blurs every comparison. The calls on stocks discussed at length, where the labels are nearly always right, show the same picture with wider ranges.
  • What finance YouTube as a whole is like. These are channels I chose, and a few of them supply most of the rows.
  • Whether the model’s hindsight plays a role. The language model that read a 2024 transcript was trained later and may know how those stocks did. I could not separate that from the ups and downs of the market.
  • What you would have earned. Every call counts as one equal position, bought at the next closing price and held. Fees, taxes and what the creators did with their own money are not in the data.

Step 6 of 6 · Act

What to take from this

If you watch finance YouTube

Treat a recommendation as an idea to look into, and take your time. The days right after a video were no better a time to buy than any other. Most recommended stocks ended behind a plain index fund ( beat the S&P 500 after 90 days).

If you rank creators, analysts or anyone else who makes predictions

Compare with the index. Counting how often a stock went up says little in a rising market: the S&P 500 itself gained % in the average 90 days of this data. Count each idea once, because of the buy calls here were repeats. Show how uncertain each number is, and leave out anyone with only a few dozen calls.

If you pull data out of text with a language model

Read a sample yourself before you trust the output. Here about three in ten “buy” labels were not recommendations at all, and I only know that because rows were checked by hand. For Vetted that means fixing the labels comes before any new feature.

Conclusion

Finance-YouTube stock calls told me very little about what a stock would do next.

Across buy calls from channels, the middle call was after 90 days, and only of the calls beat the S&P 500. The average call was ahead, by percentage points after 90 days and after 180. That average rests on a few stocks that were recommended again and again. With each idea counted once, it can no longer be told apart from zero.

It did not matter when a call was made. Stocks the creators warned about did about as well as the ones they recommended. No channel was clearly better than the others.

Two things limit how far this goes. The data covers one rising market, and the labels are right about seven times in ten. A small advantage could be hidden by that noise, but a large one would be visible in these numbers.
